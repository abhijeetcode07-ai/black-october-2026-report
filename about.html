Project notes

Make the week
legible.

A small, transparent website about a dense cluster of cyber incidents.

Why this exists

Cybersecurity reporting should be readable before it is impressive.

Black October 2026 turns seven supplied research files into a chronological, plain-English incident atlas. The point is not to flatten the technical detail. It is to give each term, date and claim a clear place to land.

Every incident page keeps the author-assigned category separate from the automated suggestions, shows what could not be confirmed, and links outward to primary, government, researcher and news sources.

Research method

Official sources first. Conflicts shown.

The content was assembled from the supplied incident research PDFs. Official and government sources are surfaced first. Where the PDFs record different figures or unresolved attribution, the page keeps the difference visible rather than silently choosing a number.

How the AI feature works

Small, browser-only, explainable.

The site uses a rule-based keyword tagger. It counts weighted topic words such as “vendor”, “certificate”, “cloud” or “agent”, then shows the matched words beside each AI-suggested tag.

It also uses TF-IDF and cosine similarity to find related reports. TF-IDF gives more weight to words that are frequent in one report but rare across the seven. Cosine similarity compares those word-score lists.

This is classical natural language processing, not a chatbot or neural network. It uses no API, no model download and no key. It counts words and does math in the browser.

Limitations

Honesty is part of the interface.

  • There are only seven documents, so similarity percentages are rough and relative.
  • The model compares words, not meanings. “Patch” and “fix” remain different words.
  • Tags depend on a hand-made keyword list and should be read as suggestions.
  • The supplied PDFs are the content boundary. Missing information is labelled instead of invented.
